Step 4: Made a searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves.

Step 4 marks a major turning point in our recovery journey. It’s the step where we stop focusing on the external world—what others have done to us, how situations have wronged us—and instead begin to look inward. It’s a deep dive into ourselves, an honest and unflinching assessment of our character, behaviors, and patterns. For many, this step can be intimidating, even frightening. But as I’ve learned, it’s also one of the most liberating steps in the entire process. In order to live a peaceful, happy, and sober life, working Steps 4 through 9 is essential—these are often called the “recovery steps” because they focus on action and change.

I won’t be providing a detailed how-to guide for working this step, as the instructions are clearly outlined in the Big Book of Alcoholics Anonymous. However, I will share some of my own experiences and insights to help guide you through this transformative process.

Facing Ourselves for the First Time
For many of us, Step 4 is the first time we’ve ever truly looked at ourselves. I’m not talking about a casual glance in the mirror, but a searching and fearless moral inventory. This step requires us to confront our character defects, our resentments, our fears, and our past actions. Before I took this step, I had spent years blaming others for my problems—my drinking, my anger, my unhappiness. But Step 4 forced me to take responsibility for my part in the chaos of my life.

One of the most difficult aspects of Step 4 was looking at my resentments. For years, my resentments had driven me toward anger and, eventually, toward the bottle. Every time I drank, I was either fueling my resentments or trying to numb them. But when I put pen to paper and began listing out my resentments, I started to see a pattern. My anger wasn’t just about what others had done to me—it was often about my own actions, my own expectations, and my own inability to let go of past hurts. I came to realize that these resentments weren’t just emotional baggage; they were driving my behavior and keeping me trapped in a cycle of anger and self-destruction.

Fear: The Silent Driver
Another major revelation for me during Step 4 was the role that fear had played in my life. Fear had gripped me for as long as I could remember, especially during my drinking days. I had been plagued by a constant fear of the unknown—what had I done the night before? Where had I been? Who had I hurt or offended? I’d wake up after a blackout, terrified to look at my phone, dreading what I might have said or done. Every drink I took seemed to add another layer of fear and uncertainty to my life.

Step 4 helped me see that these fears weren’t just about my drinking—they were deeply ingrained in my thinking and behavior. I had always been afraid of failure, afraid of rejection, afraid of not being good enough. And in response to these fears, I had tried to control everything and everyone around me. But the more I tried to control, the more chaotic my life became. Step 4 helped me name these fears, confront them, and begin the process of letting them go.

Looking at Our Sex Conduct
One area that many people shy away from in Step 4 is taking a hard look at their sex conduct. This can be uncomfortable, but it’s an essential part of the inventory process. I had to examine not only how I had treated others in my romantic and sexual relationships but also how I had treated myself. How had my behavior hurt others? How had it hurt me? For years, I had used relationships as a way to distract myself from my problems, and Step 4 forced me to confront the damage I had caused—to myself and to others. It wasn’t easy, but it was necessary.

Discovering My Part in the Chaos
One of the biggest revelations I had while working Step 4 was realizing that, in most of the difficult situations in my life, I had played a part. This wasn’t easy to accept at first. I had spent so many years convinced that I was the victim, that life had just dealt me a bad hand. But as I worked through my inventory, I started to see that, in nine out of ten cases, I had done something to provoke the situation. Whether it was being dishonest, selfish, or manipulative, I had played a role in the conflicts and chaos that surrounded me. Recognizing my part didn’t mean excusing the harmful actions of others, but it did mean taking responsibility for my own behavior.

In some cases, I found that people had hurt me without any provocation on my part. In these instances, Step 4 taught me something equally important—acceptance. I had to accept that sometimes people act out of their own pain or sickness, just as I had acted out of mine. Recognizing this helped me to begin the process of forgiveness—not just of others, but of myself.

The Power of Forgiveness
Step 4 is not just about identifying our flaws and mistakes—it’s also about healing. One of the greatest lessons I learned from this step was the power of forgiveness. The first person I needed to forgive was myself. For years, I had carried around shame and guilt for the things I had done while drinking. I had hurt people, broken trust, and caused pain. But Step 4 helped me see that I wasn’t a bad person—I was a sick person. I had been acting out of addiction, fear, and confusion. And now that I was in recovery, I had the opportunity to make amends and start living a better life.

Forgiving myself was the first step. The next step was forgiving others. This didn’t happen overnight, and it wasn’t always easy. But as I worked through my inventory and identified the people who had hurt me, I began to see them through a different lens. Like me, they were flawed, imperfect, and struggling with their own issues. In many cases, they were as sick as I had been. This realization made it easier to let go of my resentments and start the process of healing.

Later in my recovery, I learned that forgiveness isn’t just a mental or emotional decision—it’s something that requires action. In order to complete the act of forgiveness, I had to put in the footwork. This often meant making amends to those I had harmed and actively working to live in a way that reflected my commitment to change.

The Role of a Higher Power in Step 4
As I worked through Step 4, I found that my connection to my Higher Power deepened. This step enhanced my prayers and strengthened my belief that I wasn’t doing this work alone. I had always feared that taking an inventory would mean facing all the worst parts of myself, but my Higher Power helped me see that this process was about growth, not punishment. I was able to view myself with compassion and understanding, recognizing that while I had made mistakes, I also had the capacity for change.

Step 4 showed me that I wasn’t a hopeless case—I was someone who had been sick, but who now had the tools to get well. By turning my will and life over to my Higher Power, I was able to approach this step with courage and trust, knowing that the process would lead to healing and transformation.

Practical Tips for Working Step 4
When I was ready to take this step, my sponsor suggested that I make an inventory of my entire life—not just the years I had been drinking. I was surprised to discover that many of my character defects and behaviors had been present long before I ever picked up a drink. For example, I realized that I had been stealing things as a child, years before my drinking started. Recognizing these patterns helped me see that my problems weren’t just about alcohol—they were about the way I had been living my life.

If you’re preparing to work Step 4, my advice is simple: put pen to paper. Don’t worry about making your inventory neat or polished. The Big Book offers a sample inventory on page 65, and this was the format I used. I found it helpful to start by listing names in the first column—people, places, or things that I had resentments toward. From there, I filled in the rest of the information, gradually building a comprehensive picture of my past.

The most important thing is to make a beginning. Don’t get stuck trying to make your inventory perfect. Just start writing and keep going. You’ll be amazed at the insights that come when you take an honest and fearless look at yourself.
