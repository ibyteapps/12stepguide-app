Step 8: Made a list of all persons we had harmed, and became willing to make amends to them all.

Step 8 is the beginning of a powerful and healing process. It sets the stage for making amends and righting the wrongs of our past. Up until this point in the Twelve Step program, we’ve been focused on examining our own behavior, coming to terms with our defects of character, and surrendering those defects to a Higher Power. Now, in Step 8, we turn our attention outward, to the people we have harmed. This step is about accountability, honesty, and, most importantly, willingness. It is often said that the entire essence of the Twelve Step program is spiritual progress, and for true spiritual progress, we must go back and set right our wrongs. Step 8 is where that process begins.

If you have done a thorough job with your Step 4 inventory, you should already have a detailed list of the people you’ve harmed. Step 8 is about revisiting that list and taking an honest look at the ways in which we’ve caused harm to others. This step is not about dwelling on what others have done to us. It’s about owning our part in the harm we’ve caused, regardless of the actions of others. We let go of any resentments or grievances we may still be holding and focus solely on what we have done wrong.

Creating the List
When I approached Step 8, I found it to be a straightforward process. I simply copied over the names from my Step 4 inventory. In Step 4, I had already identified the people, places, and things toward which I held resentments. Now, in Step 8, I shifted my focus to the harm I had caused to these people. It was important to put aside any thoughts about what they had done to me. Step 8 isn’t about blaming or justifying—it’s about accountability.

Some of the people on my list were no longer in my life. Some had passed away, while others had moved on, and I no longer had contact with them. But I included them on my list anyway because Step 8 uses the word all. It doesn’t say, “Made a list of all persons to whom amends were possible.” It says all—and that means everyone we’ve harmed, regardless of whether they are still in our lives or whether we believe making amends is possible. The point of this step is not to make judgments about whether amends can be made yet—that comes later in Step 9. In Step 8, our focus is solely on making the list and becoming willing to make amends.

The Importance of Willingness
Willingness is the key to Step 8. It’s one thing to make a list of the people we’ve harmed—it’s another thing entirely to become willing to make amends to them. For some people, this step may come easily. But for others, the idea of making amends can feel overwhelming, even terrifying. What if they don’t forgive us? What if they react with anger or rejection? What if making amends opens old wounds that are better left in the past?

These are all valid fears, but Step 8 doesn’t ask us to take action just yet. It asks us only to become willing. We don’t have to know exactly how the amends will go or what the outcome will be. All we need is the willingness to try. And if we’re not willing, we can ask our Higher Power for help in developing that willingness. Just as we asked for help in removing our defects of character in Step 7, we can ask for help in cultivating the willingness to make amends in Step 8.

In my own experience, willingness came gradually. For some people on my list, I was immediately ready to make amends. For others, I felt resistance. There were people I had wronged who had also hurt me, and I struggled to let go of the resentment I still held toward them. But as I worked through the steps and continued to pray for willingness, I found that, over time, my resistance softened. I began to see that making amends wasn’t about them—it was about me and my spiritual growth. It was about clearing away the wreckage of my past so that I could move forward with a clean slate.

The Difference Between Step 8 and Step 9
It’s important to understand that Step 8 is about making the list and becoming willing to make amends, not about taking action just yet. Step 9 is where we actually make the amends. In Step 8, we don’t need to worry about how we will make the amends or whether they will be well-received. Our only task is to identify the people we’ve harmed and become willing to make things right.

I remember feeling a sense of relief when I realized that Step 8 wasn’t about taking action just yet. I didn’t have to confront anyone or apologize for anything right away. Instead, I had time to sit with my list, reflect on the harm I had caused, and pray for the willingness to make amends when the time came. This made the process feel more manageable, and it gave me the space I needed to truly become ready for Step 9.

Owning Our Part in the Harm
One of the most challenging aspects of Step 8 is owning our part in the harm we’ve caused. It’s easy to fall into the trap of blaming others for our actions, especially if we feel that we were wronged in some way. But Step 8 asks us to put aside those justifications and focus solely on what we have done. Even if someone else wronged us, we must take responsibility for how we responded. Did we lash out in anger? Did we manipulate or deceive them? Did we hold onto resentment and let it poison the relationship?

In my own life, there were several people I had harmed as a result of my drinking. Some of these people had also hurt me, and for a long time, I held onto the belief that I was justified in my actions. But Step 8 helped me see that my justifications didn’t matter. Regardless of what they had done, I had acted out of fear, anger, and selfishness. I had caused harm, and it was my responsibility to make things right. This realization was humbling, but it was also freeing. By owning my part in the harm, I was able to let go of the resentments that had been weighing me down.

Healing Through Willingness
While Step 8 is often seen as a precursor to Step 9, it has its own healing power. The process of making the list and becoming willing to make amends can bring a sense of closure and peace, even before the actual amends are made. By taking responsibility for our actions and becoming willing to make things right, we begin to heal the wounds we have caused—both in ourselves and in others.

For me, Step 8 was a turning point. It marked the beginning of a new chapter in my recovery, one in which I was no longer avoiding the consequences of my actions. Instead, I was facing them head-on, with the intention of making things right. This shift in perspective brought a sense of freedom that I hadn’t felt in years. I was no longer defined by the mistakes of my past—I was actively working to repair the damage and build a better future.

Practical Tips for Working Step 8
If you’re approaching Step 8, my advice is to keep it simple. Start by revisiting your Step 4 inventory and identifying the people you’ve harmed. Write down their names, and then take some time to reflect on the specific ways in which you caused harm. Don’t worry about what they did to you—that’s not the focus of this step. Instead, focus on your actions and your part in the situation.

Next, pray for willingness. If you’re not ready to make amends to certain people, that’s okay. Ask your Higher Power for help in developing that willingness, and trust that it will come in time. Remember, Step 8 is about progress, not perfection. As long as you’re willing to try, you’re on the right path.
