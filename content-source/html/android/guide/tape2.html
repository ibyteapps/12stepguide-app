Step 1: We admitted we were powerless over alcohol—that our lives had become unmanageable.

If you are reading this, there's a strong chance that you have already taken the first step, even if you might not realize it yet. Step 1 is more than just an admission of defeat—it’s a profound realization that consists of two separate, yet interconnected parts: our powerlessness over alcohol and the unmanageability of our lives. Let’s break this step down to gain a deeper understanding of both aspects.

Admitting powerlessness over alcohol:
This part of Step 1 is crucial because it is the first time we confront the reality that we are not in control of our drinking. For many, it’s not just about knowing, but truly admitting on a soul-deep level that no amount of willpower, self-control, or promises to others can keep us from picking up that first drink. When I first came to AA, I was bombarded by stories that mirrored my own. I had convinced myself for years that no one could understand my struggle. Yet here I was, listening to people recount experiences that could have been pages from my own life. They described the same feelings of shame, regret, and helplessness. They shared the same patterns of behavior—the lies, the failed attempts to quit, the promises to loved ones, and the inevitable failure to keep those promises.

It was liberating, in a way, to realize that I was not alone. I had been convinced that I was the only one who drank the way I did, that no one else could possibly understand. But in those rooms, surrounded by people who had walked the same path, I realized that my struggle was not unique—and that gave me hope. It wasn't just about admitting powerlessness; it was about finding relief in the fact that others had felt this powerlessness and had found a solution. Admitting powerlessness meant accepting that once I picked up the first drink, I couldn't stop. The physical craving would set in, and the mental obsession took over. No amount of promises or resolutions could break that cycle. By admitting this, I freed myself from the constant struggle to control something that was beyond my control.

It’s important to remember that this admission is not about weakness; it’s about acknowledging a simple truth. For people like us, alcohol is something we cannot safely consume—not now, not ever. This surrender is paradoxically empowering, as it opens the door to a new way of life, one where we are no longer trapped in the endless cycle of drinking and regret.

That our lives had become unmanageable:
This second part of Step 1 often gets less attention, but it is equally vital. For some of us, our lives were unmanageable long before we ever picked up a drink. For others, the unmanageability only became apparent as the drinking spiraled out of control. Either way, it is crucial to understand that these two parts of Step 1 are connected, yet they are not always experienced in the same way or at the same time.

For me, the unmanageability of my life was evident even after I stopped drinking. I had managed to put down the bottle, but my emotional, mental, and spiritual life was still in chaos. I was constantly irritable, restless, and discontent. No matter how much sobriety time I accumulated, I couldn’t find peace. I was still trying to control everything and everyone around me. My relationships were strained, my work life was stressful, and I was filled with resentment. I didn’t realize that the unmanageability wasn’t just about the external mess—broken relationships, job loss, or financial ruin—but also about the internal turmoil I was experiencing.

It wasn’t until I began working the steps, really working them with the guidance of a sponsor, that my life began to transform. One of the most important realizations I had was that my unmanageability wasn’t solely tied to drinking; it was a symptom of my deeper issues. My inability to accept life on life’s terms, my desire to control everything, and my constant search for something outside myself to make me feel okay—all of these contributed to my unmanageable life. Only through surrender and working the program did I begin to find balance, and gradually, my life became manageable. It didn’t happen overnight, but one day at a time, things started to fall into place. I became more peaceful, more accepting, and more capable of handling life’s challenges.

Step 1 is not a working step; it’s a conclusion of the mind.
When we admit we are powerless over alcohol, we aren’t necessarily “doing” anything. We are acknowledging a truth that has been staring us in the face for a long time. We come to this realization through our experiences in the rooms, through hearing others share their stories, and through seeing the common threads that connect all of us who suffer from alcoholism. This step is often referred to as the only step that can be taken 100%. Unlike the other 11 steps, which we work to the best of our ability, Step 1 is absolute. Once we fully accept it, we don’t need to revisit it or question it. It becomes the foundation upon which we build the rest of our recovery.

But make no mistake—this doesn’t mean that Step 1 is easy. For many of us, it’s the hardest part of the program. Admitting powerlessness goes against everything we’ve been taught. We’ve been conditioned to believe that we should be able to control ourselves, that we can will ourselves into better behavior. But alcoholism is different. It requires a different approach—one that begins with surrender.

As they say in the rooms, “Easy does it, but do it.” Take your time with this step, but don’t hesitate to take it fully. It’s the key to unlocking a new way of life, free from the obsession and compulsion to drink. It’s the beginning of a journey that, if you allow it, will lead to a life that is not just manageable, but full of peace, joy, and purpose. Step 1 is where it all begins.
