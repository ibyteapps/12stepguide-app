Tradition 8: Alcoholics Anonymous should remain forever nonprofessional, but our service centers may employ special workers.

Tradition 8 ensures that Alcoholics Anonymous (A.A.) remains a nonprofessional fellowship where the primary focus is on helping fellow alcoholics without expectation of financial compensation. At the same time, this tradition recognizes the need for certain services that may require professional expertise, and allows A.A. to hire special workers for those specific roles. This tradition strikes a delicate balance between preserving the spirit of volunteerism and ensuring that A.A. can function effectively on a larger scale.

The Spirit of Volunteerism in A.A.
At its core, A.A. is a program built on the principle of freely giving away what we have received. The program of recovery was given to each of us for free, and in turn, we are expected to pass it on to others without expecting any form of payment. This is a cornerstone of A.A.’s ethos and is reflected in the phrase, "You have to give it away to keep it." By sharing our experience, strength, and hope with others, we maintain our own sobriety, while helping others find theirs.

One of the beautiful aspects of A.A. is that no one gets paid to carry the message. When we participate in 12-step work—whether it’s sponsoring someone, speaking at a meeting, or offering guidance to a newcomer—we do so because we want to help, not because we expect financial gain. The idea of being paid to help another alcoholic would undermine the selflessness that is at the heart of A.A.’s recovery process.

In my early days of sobriety, I was told that A.A. should remain nonprofessional, meaning that no one should ever be paid for doing 12-step work. This didn’t make much sense to me at first. I thought, "Why shouldn’t someone be compensated for their time and effort?" But over time, I began to understand the deeper wisdom behind this tradition. The paradox is real—when we give freely without expecting anything in return, we receive so much more. Helping another alcoholic is one of the most rewarding experiences in recovery, and the absence of money in the equation ensures that our motives remain pure.

A.A. and the Need for Special Workers
While A.A. remains a nonprofessional fellowship, it does acknowledge the need for certain services that require professional expertise. This is where the second part of Tradition 8 comes into play: "but our service centers may employ special workers." A.A. groups, intergroups, and service centers sometimes require the help of individuals with specialized skills—people who are not necessarily members of A.A. These special workers can be paid for their expertise, allowing A.A. to function efficiently while remaining true to its nonprofessional principles.

One such experience I had involved ordering custom-printed stationery for the fellowship from a printing press. The printer wasn’t a member of A.A., but the fellowship needed his services to produce high-quality materials that we could use to carry the message. This is a perfect example of how Tradition 8 allows for the hiring of special workers to meet specific needs, without violating the principle of nonprofessionalism in A.A.’s core activities.

Professionalism in Service, Not in Recovery
Another memorable experience I had was during the world convention in Atlanta, where approximately 65,000 people attended. I volunteered at the event, and during my time there, I learned that A.A. had hired an event management company to help organize and execute the convention at the Georgia Dome. The fellowship simply didn’t have the expertise to manage an event of such magnitude with the professionalism required. Hiring a professional event management company was necessary to ensure the event ran smoothly.

What struck me during this convention was how Tradition 8 came to life. While A.A. members were responsible for sharing the message, leading meetings, and volunteering, the logistical and organizational aspects of the event were handled by professionals. This separation allowed A.A. to focus on its primary purpose—carrying the message of recovery—while the professionals handled the tasks that required specialized skills, such as event coordination, security, and logistics.

This distinction is crucial: A.A. remains nonprofessional in its core mission—helping alcoholics recover—but it can still employ professionals to handle the technical or administrative aspects that fall outside the scope of recovery work. Whether it’s hiring a cleaner to maintain the cleanliness of a meeting space, employing a computer operator for office work, or enlisting a legal adviser for specialized advice, A.A. service centers can utilize outside professionals without compromising the fellowship’s principles.

Why Nonprofessionalism Matters
The principle of nonprofessionalism in A.A. serves several important purposes. First, it ensures that the focus of A.A. remains on the spiritual aspect of recovery rather than turning it into a transactional or commercial endeavor. The moment people start getting paid to carry the message, the dynamic changes. What was once a selfless act of service could become a job, and with that shift comes the risk of diluting the spiritual foundation of the program.

Second, nonprofessionalism maintains the equality of all members within the fellowship. No one member is "better" or "more qualified" than another to help an alcoholic recover. Whether you have 30 years of sobriety or 30 days, your experience is valuable and can help others. By removing financial incentives, A.A. fosters an environment where every member is on equal footing, united by their shared experience with alcoholism rather than their professional qualifications.

Finally, nonprofessionalism keeps A.A. accessible to everyone, regardless of their financial situation. A.A. meetings are free, and no one is required to pay for membership or services. This makes A.A. a welcoming and inclusive space where anyone with a desire to stop drinking can find help, without the worry of financial barriers. By declining to professionalize its services, A.A. ensures that the program remains available to all alcoholics, regardless of their economic status.

The Role of Service Centers and Special Workers
A.A.’s service centers, such as intergroups, regional offices, and the General Service Office, are essential to the fellowship’s ability to function on a larger scale. These centers often handle administrative tasks, coordinate events, distribute literature, and provide support to local groups. While A.A. members volunteer their time and energy to serve the fellowship, there are instances when specialized skills are required—skills that may not exist within the membership. In such cases, it’s perfectly acceptable to hire special workers to perform these tasks.

For example, I once visited an A.A. service office that employed computer operators, receptionists, and other staff who weren’t members of the fellowship. These individuals were paid for their work, but they weren’t involved in 12-step work. Their job was to handle the day-to-day operations of the service center, ensuring that the fellowship’s larger organizational needs were met. This arrangement allows A.A. to maintain a high level of professionalism in its operations while staying true to its nonprofessional roots when it comes to carrying the message of recovery.

Freely Given, Freely Give
The principle of nonprofessionalism in A.A. is deeply tied to the idea that recovery is a gift—something that was freely given to us, and that we must freely give to others. This concept is encapsulated in the phrase, "Freely ye have received, freely give!" When we give our time, energy, and experience to help another alcoholic, we do so because we know that our own sobriety depends on it. The paradox is real: by giving away what we have received, we keep it. This principle ensures that A.A. remains a fellowship of equals, bound together by a common purpose rather than financial incentives.

Conclusion: Balancing Volunteerism and Professionalism
Tradition 8 strikes a vital balance between maintaining A.A.’s spirit of volunteerism and acknowledging the need for professional expertise in certain areas. While A.A. members are never paid for carrying the message or doing 12-step work, the fellowship can employ special workers for tasks that require specialized skills. This allows A.A. to function effectively on a global scale, without compromising the core principles that make it unique.

By remaining nonprofessional in its recovery work, A.A. ensures that the program remains accessible to all, that the spiritual foundation of the fellowship remains intact, and that members continue to give freely without expectation of reward. At the same time, by hiring special workers when necessary, A.A. ensures that its service centers can operate efficiently and professionally, providing the support needed to keep the fellowship running smoothly.

Freely ye have received, freely give!
