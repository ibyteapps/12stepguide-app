# Step 9: Made direct amends to such people wherever possible, except when to do so would injure them or others.

Step 9 is one of the most transformative steps in the Twelve Step program. It is where the healing begins—not just for us, but for those we have harmed. After identifying the people we’ve hurt in Step 8 and becoming willing to make amends, Step 9 is where we take action. It is about taking responsibility for our past actions and setting things right. This step isn’t just about saying "I’m sorry." It’s about making concrete efforts to repair the harm we’ve done and showing, through our actions, that we are committed to living differently.

In my experience, Step 9 was the most effective step in my recovery when it came to deflating my ego and helping me grow in humility. I had always been quick to apologize after my drunken episodes, but those apologies were often hollow. They were my way of trying to smooth things over without truly taking responsibility for my actions. When I entered recovery, I wanted to jump straight into Step 9 and start making amends on the very first day. But my sponsor wisely suggested that I work the steps in the right numerical order. He reminded me that Step 9 is numbered 9 for a reason: it requires a foundation of sobriety and a thorough understanding of our wrongs before we can approach those we’ve harmed.

## The Importance of Working the Steps in Order

As eager as I was to make amends right away, I quickly realized the wisdom in working the steps in order. When we’re newly sober, we’re often still raw—emotionally, mentally, and spiritually. We’re still learning to navigate life without alcohol, and we may not yet have the clarity or stability needed to make amends effectively. By the time I reached Step 9, I had some sobriety under my belt, and that made all the difference. People could see that I was serious about my recovery, that I wasn’t just making empty apologies like I had in the past. I had done the work of identifying my defects in Step 4, becoming willing to let go of them in Step 6, and asking my Higher Power to remove them in Step 7. This gave me the foundation I needed to approach Step 9 with humility and sincerity.

## Seeking Guidance from My Sponsor

One of the most important pieces of advice I received during Step 9 was to consult my sponsor about every amend. Some amends were straightforward, but others were more complicated. I found that discussing each situation with my sponsor helped me navigate the process more effectively. For some amends, I wasn’t sure if making direct contact was the right approach, and my sponsor was able to offer valuable guidance. For example, I had stolen books from a library in my childhood, but returning to that library would have put my family at risk, as the owner had a reputation for causing harm. My sponsor suggested that, instead of making a direct amend, I could make a donation to a charity. This allowed me to make amends without causing further harm.

Not every amend could be made directly, but every time I made an amend, whether direct or indirect, I experienced a little more freedom. It was as though each amend lifted a weight from my shoulders, allowing me to move forward with a clearer conscience and a lighter heart.

## Making Amends to the Deceased

One of the most challenging aspects of Step 9 was making amends to people who were no longer alive. My grandfather was one of the people I owed amends to, but he had passed away before I got sober. I had wronged him long before I even picked up a drink, and the guilt I carried over those actions weighed heavily on me. My sponsor suggested that I write a letter to my grandfather, explaining the wrongs I had done and expressing my desire to make amends. Once I had written the letter, I was instructed to burn it, symbolically releasing the guilt and making peace with the past. This process was deeply healing for me. Even though I couldn’t make a direct amend, I felt a sense of closure and relief, knowing that I had made an effort to set things right in my heart.

## Facing Fear and Resistance in Making Amends

Not all amends were easy to make. Some of them stirred up fear and resistance within me. There were people I had wronged so long ago that I doubted they would even remember the incident. For example, I had stolen a book from a school friend over 20 years ago, and I hesitated to reach out, thinking it was ancient history. But my sponsor reminded me that if it was still playing on my mind, it was something I needed to address. So, I called my friend, who lived in a different country. I didn’t mention anything about the AA program or my alcoholism—I simply told him that I had stolen the book all those years ago and that I wanted to make amends as part of my commitment to living a more spiritual life. To my surprise, my friend received the amend graciously, and we’ve since reconnected. It was a humbling experience that reminded me that no matter how long ago the harm was done, making amends is always worthwhile.

## The Limits of Making Amends

There were some people on my Step 8 list whom I couldn’t find, but I made a conscious decision to remain willing to make amends if I ever crossed paths with them again. The Big Book of Alcoholics Anonymous tells us to make amends wherever possible, but it also acknowledges that there will be cases where direct amends are not possible or would cause harm. In those situations, willingness is key. Even if we can’t make the amend right now, we can remain open to the possibility of doing so in the future.

## The Healing Power of Amends

Making amends is not just about apologizing or "making things right"—it’s about healing. For me, Step 9 marked the beginning of a deeper spiritual journey. The act of making amends allowed me to let go of the guilt and shame I had been carrying for years. It allowed me to repair damaged relationships and restore my integrity. Each time I made an amend, I felt a little more free, a little more connected to my Higher Power, and a little more at peace with myself.

In the Big Book, right after Step 9, it says, "*We have entered the world of the Spirit*." For me, this line took on a new meaning once I completed this step. After making amends, I felt a deeper connection to my Higher Power—a sense that I was no longer just talking to God, but experiencing direct contact with a power greater than myself. It was as though my conscience had been cleared, and I could now hear the voice of my Higher Power more clearly.

## Practical Tips for Working Step 9

If you’re approaching Step 9, my advice is to take your time and work closely with your sponsor. Every amend is different, and it’s important to approach each one thoughtfully and with guidance. Not every amend will be easy, but each one will bring you a little closer to freedom.

When making amends, it’s important to remember that you’re not responsible for how the other person reacts. Some people will accept your amends graciously, while others may respond with anger or indifference. But regardless of their response, the act of making the amend is what matters. You are taking responsibility for your actions and showing a commitment to living differently.

If you can’t find someone to make amends to, or if making direct amends would cause harm, consider alternative ways to make amends. Writing a letter, making a donation, or doing service work can all be meaningful ways to set things right.

Finally, remember that Step 9 is not about perfection—it’s about progress. As long as you are willing to make amends and are taking steps to do so, you are on the right path.
