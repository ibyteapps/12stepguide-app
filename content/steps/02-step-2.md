# Step 2: Came to believe that a Power greater than ourselves could restore us to sanity.

Just like Step 1, Step 2 is not a working step in the traditional sense. This means that you don’t need to actively “do” something for this step to happen. Instead, it’s a conclusion of the mind—a belief that forms as you continue to listen and identify with others in meetings. It’s about coming to an understanding, a shift in perception, and ultimately, faith in the possibility of change.

## Understanding Insanity:

To truly take Step 2, you first need to accept that there is an element of insanity in your life. This can be a difficult pill to swallow. It’s one thing to admit that we have lost control over alcohol, but to acknowledge that our thinking and behavior have been fundamentally irrational or insane is much harder. For me, this didn’t come easily. I struggled with the idea that I was somehow “insane” until I heard someone in a meeting explain it in simple terms: “Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting a different result.”

That was when it clicked for me. Every time I picked up that first drink, I convinced myself that *this* time would be different. I would drink responsibly, I wouldn’t go overboard, and I wouldn’t wake up in a mess of regret. But every single time, I ended up in the same place—drunk, ashamed, and wondering why I had let it happen again. I had tried this experiment a thousand times, always expecting a different outcome, but the result was always the same. That’s the definition of insanity. The moment I accepted that, Step 2 started to make sense.

I heard another person in a meeting say, “Not every time I got drunk did I get into trouble, but every time I got into trouble, I was drunk.” That hit me hard. It made me realize that alcohol was not just part of my problem—it was *the* problem. It was the common thread running through every bad decision, every failed relationship, and every crisis I faced. Alcohol was the driving force behind my insane thinking and behavior. Once I could see that clearly, the idea that I needed help from something greater than myself became much more approachable.

## Relapse and Insanity:

My own journey had its share of relapses, and each one was another act of insanity. After five weeks of sobriety, I convinced myself that I had cracked the code, that I had solved my drinking problem. I stopped going to meetings and, unsurprisingly, I relapsed—hard. That relapse lasted for three weeks, and it was a dark time for me. But even during that stretch, a part of me knew what I was doing was insane. I was fully aware that I was an alcoholic, I had voluntarily gone to meetings, and yet I still picked up that drink. It was madness.

I consider myself incredibly lucky that the relapse lasted only three weeks because, in this fellowship, I’ve seen people relapse and never make it back. Some of them have since passed away, succumbing to the disease that we all face. It’s a stark reminder that alcoholism is a matter of life and death, and that without a Power greater than ourselves, the consequences can be fatal.

## Examples of Insanity in Others:

I think often about a close friend of mine in the fellowship. He had woken up from a coma after experiencing liver failure. Most people would consider that a clear sign that their drinking had gone too far. But he didn’t. He continued drinking for another year, completely convinced that he didn’t have a problem. It wasn’t until later that he came to believe in a Higher Power and found sobriety. Today, he is a valued member of both the fellowship and society, but it took him a long time to reach that point. His story is a powerful example of just how strong denial and insanity can be. Even in the face of life-threatening consequences, the alcoholic mind can still find ways to rationalize continued drinking.

## Choosing a Higher Power:

For many, including myself, the concept of a Power greater than ourselves is one of the biggest hurdles in this program. I struggled for a long time to find a Higher Power that made sense to me. Initially, I used the rooms of the fellowship as my Higher Power. I went to meetings almost every day, and that collective wisdom and support became my strength. It was through those meetings that I began to understand that my Higher Power didn’t have to be a religious figure or a traditional deity. The beauty of the Twelve Steps is that you get to choose a Higher Power of *your* understanding, and it’s a deeply personal process.

This program is spiritual, not religious. That’s an important distinction. You don’t have to subscribe to any specific doctrine, and no one is telling you what to believe. All you need is the willingness to accept that there is something greater than yourself that can restore you to sanity. For some, this is God as they’ve always understood Him. For others, it might be nature, the universe, or simply the fellowship itself. The important thing is not what you call your Higher Power, but that you are open to the idea that there is something beyond you that can help you recover.

## The Promises and Step 9:

In the “Big Book” of Alcoholics Anonymous, the Promises that follow Step 9 are incredibly powerful. One of the most striking promises is: “*We will suddenly realize that God is doing for us what we could not do for ourselves*.” For me, this was a turning point in my journey. It wasn’t until I started working Step 9 that I began to see this promise come true in my own life. I looked back and realized that a Higher Power had been present all along, guiding me, even when I wasn’t aware of it. It was only through working the steps that I became fully conscious of this presence.

We all have different experiences when it comes to finding a Higher Power. Some people come to believe quickly, while for others, it takes time. There is no right or wrong way to go about it. The key is to keep moving forward, to keep working the steps, and to remain open to the possibility of change. As long as you continue on this path, the Promises will come true in your life.

## A Program of Action:

Step 2 teaches us that belief is not enough on its own; it must be followed by action. This is a program of action, and as long as you continue to work the steps, the spiritual principles will begin to manifest in your life. You don’t have to have everything figured out right away. Just keep taking the next right step, and trust that your Higher Power will guide you. Over time, you’ll start to notice the changes—not only in your external circumstances but in your internal state of being. You’ll feel more peace, more clarity, and more sanity.

As the saying goes: “**Religion is following the messenger; spirituality is following the message**.” Step 2 is about embracing that message—that a Power greater than ourselves can restore us to sanity. Once you accept this, you open the door to a new way of living, one that is free from the chaos of alcoholism and full of hope for the future.
