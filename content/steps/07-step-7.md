# Step 7: Humbly asked Him to remove our shortcomings.

Step 7 marks a deepening of the surrender that began in Step 6. While Step 6 is about becoming entirely ready to have our defects of character removed, Step 7 is where we actively ask our Higher Power to remove them. This is not a passive request—it’s a humble acknowledgment that we cannot remove these defects on our own. We need help. And that help comes from a power greater than ourselves. But Step 7 isn’t just about asking—it’s also about living with a spirit of humility, recognizing that we are not the center of the universe, and that true growth comes from being teachable, no matter how far along we are in recovery.

## The Importance of Humility

The first word of this step, "humbly," is key to understanding its deeper meaning. To be humble is to have a realistic sense of our place in the world. It means recognizing that we are not the most important person in the room, that we are not all-knowing, and that we cannot control everything around us. Humility is the opposite of pride, one of the most destructive defects of character. Pride tells us that we can fix ourselves, that we don’t need help, that we are better than others. But humility acknowledges the truth: we are flawed, we make mistakes, and we need help. It’s through this humility that we can ask our Higher Power to remove our shortcomings.

When I first approached Step 7, I struggled with the concept of humility. For years, my pride had been my armor. It was what I used to protect myself from the world, to pretend that I was in control when, in reality, my life was spiraling out of control. Admitting that I needed help, that I couldn’t fix myself, felt like weakness. But as I worked through the steps, I began to see that humility wasn’t about weakness—it was about strength. It took strength to admit that I didn’t have all the answers, that I needed guidance, and that I was willing to ask for help.

## Recognizing Our Shortcomings

In Step 4, we made a searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves, identifying our defects of character—the traits and behaviors that have caused harm to ourselves and others. In Step 6, we became entirely ready to let go of these defects. Now, in Step 7, we humbly ask our Higher Power to remove them. But what does it mean to ask for our shortcomings to be removed? For me, this step wasn’t about waking up one day and being magically free of all my defects. It was about asking for the willingness and the strength to confront these defects as they arise, and to respond to them in new, healthier ways.

The *12 & 12* mentions the seven deadly sins: Pride, Greed, Lust, Anger, Gluttony, Envy, and Sloth. These sins, or character defects, are deeply ingrained in all of us. We cannot simply rid ourselves of them overnight. They are part of the human condition. But through Step 7, we ask for help in managing these defects, in becoming more aware of when they crop up, and in learning to respond differently. In my own experience, I found that working on these defects required conscious effort. I began each day by saying the Step 7 prayer and asking my Higher Power for help in dealing with my character defects. My prayer was simple: “*God, please help me deal with my character defects*.”

Each day, I chose two of the seven deadly sins to focus on. I made a conscious decision to be aware of these defects as they arose in my thoughts, feelings, and actions. When pride started creeping in, I tried to practice humility. When anger flared up, I paused and reminded myself to stay calm. With time and practice, I began to notice a real change in my behavior.

## The Process of Letting Go

One of the most significant defects I worked on was my anger. For a long time, anger had been my default response whenever things didn’t go my way. Whether it was a small inconvenience or a major setback, my temper would flare up, and I’d start ranting and complaining. But as I began working Step 7, I made a conscious effort to address this defect. Every day, I prayed for help in dealing with my anger. And every day, I tried to act in new, healthier ways when I felt that anger rising.

This didn’t happen overnight. It took a full year of daily effort before I noticed a significant change. But gradually, I began to control my anger instead of letting it control me. I found new ways to express my frustration without lashing out, and I learned to let go of the need for everything to go my way. Today, people who know me say they haven’t seen me angry in a very long time, and for that, I am deeply grateful. This change didn’t happen because I willed it to happen—it happened because I asked my Higher Power for help, and I stayed willing to let go of my old behaviors.

## Character Defects vs. Shortcomings

If you’re confused by the difference between "character defects" and "shortcomings," you’re not alone. The terms are often used interchangeably in the program, and for good reason—they essentially mean the same thing. Bill W., the co-founder of Alcoholics Anonymous, had a particular way with words, and he didn’t like to repeat himself. So, throughout the Big Book and the *12 & 12*, you’ll find different terms being used to describe the same concepts. In the context of Step 7, both "defects of character" and "shortcomings" refer to the flaws and behaviors that we’ve identified in earlier steps, and that we now humbly ask to be removed.

## The Power of Practice

One of the key lessons I’ve learned from Step 7 is that practice makes progress. The more I consciously work on my defects of character, the easier it becomes to recognize and address them when they arise. When I first started working Step 7, it was difficult to catch myself in the moment. My anger, pride, or envy would flare up before I even realized what was happening. But with practice, I became more aware of these defects as they surfaced. I learned to pause, take a breath, and ask for help in responding differently.

Over time, I began to see that these defects didn’t have the same power over me that they once did. I wasn’t perfect—I still got angry, I still felt pride, I still struggled with envy—but I was no longer a prisoner to these emotions. By humbly asking for help, I had opened myself up to real change.

## True Humility and Being Teachable

At the heart of Step 7 is the concept of true humility. True humility means staying teachable, regardless of how much we think we know. It means recognizing that no matter how far we’ve come in recovery, there is always room for growth. There is always something to learn, always a defect to work on, always a way to improve. When we approach life with this kind of humility, we become open to the guidance of our Higher Power. We stop trying to control everything, and we start trusting that we are on the right path, even if we don’t always know what that path looks like.

In my own journey, staying teachable has been one of the most important aspects of recovery. There have been times when I’ve felt like I had everything figured out, only to be humbled by life’s challenges. But each time I’ve returned to Step 7 and asked for help, I’ve found that I’m able to grow a little more. I’ve learned to embrace the process, knowing that true change comes not from striving for perfection, but from staying willing and open to the lessons of life.

## Practical Tips for Working Step 7

If you’re working on Step 7, my advice is to keep it simple. Start each day with a prayer, asking your Higher Power for help in removing your shortcomings. You don’t have to tackle all your defects at once. Choose one or two to focus on each day, and practice being mindful of them as they arise. When you notice a defect creeping in—whether it’s pride, anger, envy, or something else—pause, take a breath, and ask for help in responding differently.

Remember that this is a lifelong process. You won’t be rid of your defects overnight, and that’s okay. The goal is not perfection—it’s progress. As long as you stay humble, stay teachable, and keep asking for help, you’re moving in the right direction.
