# Step 12: Having had a spiritual awakening as the result of these steps, we tried to carry this message to alcoholics, and to practice these principles in all our affairs.

Step 12 is both the culmination of the Twelve Step program and the beginning of a new way of life. It’s the step where everything comes together—the spiritual awakening we’ve experienced, the responsibility to share that experience with others, and the commitment to practice the principles we’ve learned in every aspect of our daily lives. It’s made up of three distinct parts, each with its own profound meaning and purpose.

## 1. "Having had a spiritual awakening as the result of these steps"

The first part of Step 12 is a powerful promise: through working the previous 11 steps, we will experience a spiritual awakening. This isn’t something we have to chase or force—it’s a natural outcome of the process. For me, this awakening wasn’t a sudden, dramatic event. It was more like a gradual realization, a shift in how I saw myself, my life, and the world around me. As I worked the steps, I slowly let go of self-centeredness, fear, and resentment, and I began to live with more humility, compassion, and trust in my Higher Power. This spiritual awakening is different for everyone, but it always brings with it a sense of peace, clarity, and purpose that was absent in the chaos of active alcoholism.

The promise of spiritual awakening doesn’t mean that we become perfect or that we’re free from struggles. Rather, it means that we’ve gained a new perspective on life. We see that we are part of something greater than ourselves, and we understand that our actions and attitudes have a ripple effect on the people around us. The gift of this spiritual awakening is not just for us—it’s something we’re meant to share with others, which brings us to the second part of Step 12.

## 2. "We tried to carry this message to alcoholics"

Carrying the message of recovery to other alcoholics is at the heart of Step 12. It’s the way we give back what was so freely given to us. When I first got sober, I thought that once I had found sobriety, I could just focus on my own life and be done with it. But the truth is, this gift of sobriety is something that only grows stronger when it’s shared. The paradox of recovery is that in order to keep it, we must give it away.

Carrying the message doesn’t always mean sponsoring someone right away or leading a meeting—it can start with something as simple as sharing our story. For a long time, I didn’t realize that just talking about my experience in meetings was a way of carrying the message. Every time I shared about my struggles, my victories, and my journey, I was helping someone else hear what recovery could look like for them. Sometimes, we carry the message just by being present at a meeting, showing newcomers that recovery is possible.

As I grew in sobriety, I started doing more formal service work. My sponsor gradually guided me into service opportunities, whether it was making coffee at meetings, setting up chairs, or eventually sponsoring others. One of the most important lessons I learned was that I cannot stop anyone else’s drinking. I am powerless over their alcoholism, just as I was over my own. But what I can do is share my experience, strength, and hope, and let them know that recovery is possible if they’re willing to take the steps. When someone asks me if I can help them stop drinking, my answer is always the same: "I can’t stop your drinking, but I can help you get to a meeting where you might hear something that will keep you sober for one more day."

## The Power of Personal Experience

When carrying the message, I’ve learned that it’s essential to speak from my own experience. It’s not about telling someone what they should do or how they should live their life—it’s about sharing what worked for me. My opinions and advice are secondary to the experience I’ve had in recovery. Alcoholics are more likely to connect with someone who has been through what they’ve been through, who understands the pain, the fear, and the desperation of addiction. That’s why my story is the most valuable tool I have when carrying the message.

I also learned early on that trying to carry the message to someone who is still drunk or in active addiction is often ineffective. They may not remember the conversation when they sober up, and it’s difficult to reach someone who is not yet ready to hear the message. That’s why it’s important to wait until they are sober and receptive before attempting to talk to them about recovery.

## 3. "And to practice these principles in all our affairs"

The third part of Step 12 is where we take the principles we’ve learned in the steps and apply them to every aspect of our lives. Recovery isn’t something we only do in meetings or with our sponsor—it’s a way of life. Practicing these principles means being honest, humble, compassionate, and self-aware in all of our relationships and daily activities. It means taking personal responsibility for our actions, admitting when we’re wrong, and continuing to grow spiritually.

For me, practicing these principles means that I strive to be the best version of myself in every area of my life. It doesn’t mean I’m perfect—far from it—but it does mean that I’m committed to growing and improving. Whether I’m at work, at home, or in social situations, I try to bring the same humility, honesty, and openness that I’ve learned in recovery. If I make a mistake, I admit it. If I hurt someone, I make amends. If I’m feeling resentful or fearful, I take time to reflect, pray, and ask my Higher Power for guidance.

One of the hardest lessons I’ve learned is that practicing these principles is a lifelong journey. In my early days of sobriety, I wanted to rush through the steps and declare that I had "completed" them. But as my sponsor reminded me, recovery is not about completing the steps—it’s about living them every day. It took me over three years to truly understand this, and even now, I continue to take the steps every day.

## Studying the Traditions and Concepts

As I continued to grow in recovery, I realized that Step 12 was just the beginning of my spiritual journey. Alongside the steps, I also began studying the Twelve Traditions and Twelve Concepts with my sponsor. The Traditions and Concepts provide additional guidance for living a life of service and humility, both within and outside of the fellowship. They remind me that recovery is not just about my individual journey—it’s about being part of a larger community, working together for the common good.

## Living with Humility and Gratitude

Today, I am far from perfect, but I am finally comfortable in my own skin. I no longer strive for perfection, and I’ve learned to accept myself as I am. I continue to grow, to learn, and to practice the principles of the program in all my affairs. The spiritual awakening I’ve experienced as a result of the steps has given me a new perspective on life—one that is grounded in humility, gratitude, and service to others.

By carrying the message and practicing these principles, I’ve found a life that is rich with meaning and purpose. I hope that my experience, strength, and hope will inspire you to take your own journey through the Twelve Steps, with the guidance of your Higher Power and your sponsor.
