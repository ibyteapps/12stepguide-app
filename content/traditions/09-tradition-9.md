# Tradition 9: A.A., as such, ought never be organized; but we may create service boards or committees directly responsible to those they serve.

At first glance, Tradition 9 may seem puzzling, even contradictory. How can a worldwide fellowship as large and impactful as Alcoholics Anonymous (A.A.) function without being organized? Surely, with hundreds of thousands of groups across the globe, some form of organization must be necessary to keep things running smoothly. And yet, Tradition 9 specifically states that A.A. "ought never be organized." To an outsider or a newcomer, this might sound like chaos waiting to happen. But in reality, Tradition 9 encapsulates the genius of how A.A. operates—by embracing simplicity, flexibility, and a spirit of self-governance that keeps the focus squarely on recovery.

## Why A.A. Resists Traditional Organization

The founders of A.A., including Bill W. and Dr. Bob, understood early on that alcoholics do not respond well to being controlled or dictated to, either individually or collectively. Many of us, myself included, have a rebellious streak. When someone tries to force a solution on us, our instinctive reaction is to resist—even when we know deep down that the advice is sound. It’s a paradox of human nature, but particularly common among alcoholics. Bill W. once remarked that alcoholics, more than most people, are averse to being told what to do. We are hardwired to reject authority, and A.A.'s early members learned this lesson through trial and error.

In the early days of A.A., as the fellowship began to grow, there was talk of creating a formal organizational structure. Some members thought that A.A. needed officers, boards of directors, and rigid rules to govern its operations. After all, most successful organizations in the world had such structures. But the founders quickly realized that imposing such a top-down structure on A.A. would be a disaster. Alcoholics, by nature, bristle at control and authority. If A.A. were to impose strict organizational rules, it would likely lead to division and rebellion among the members. Instead, they found that the best way to foster cooperation and commitment was to offer guidance, not rules, and to give members the freedom to manage their own affairs.

This principle is reflected in the fact that A.A. groups are autonomous, as outlined in Tradition 4. Each group is free to run its meetings and handle its affairs as it sees fit, provided it doesn’t harm A.A. as a whole. This autonomy is one of A.A.’s greatest strengths, as it allows groups to adapt to the unique needs of their members while still adhering to the core principles of the program.

## Liberty Over Organization: The Paradox That Works

The paradox of Tradition 9 is that when people are given the freedom to operate without rigid control, they often adhere to suggested guidelines even more faithfully than they would under strict rules. This is particularly true in A.A., where members have a deep personal investment in their recovery and in the success of their group. When no one is dictating what they must do, members take personal responsibility for their actions and often follow the suggestions with more commitment and enthusiasm. This sense of ownership and self-direction is what allows A.A. to thrive without formal organization.

I have seen this paradox play out many times in my own experience with A.A. When I first got sober, I was skeptical about how A.A. could function without leaders or formal structure. How could a group of alcoholics, who by their nature tend to resist authority, manage to stay on track without someone taking charge? But as I attended more meetings and became involved in service work, I began to understand the beauty of Tradition 9. I saw how, when left to their own devices, individuals and groups in A.A. often produced better results than if they had been operating under a set of rigid rules. We don’t need anyone telling us what to do because we’re personally invested in making it work.

A.A. operates more like a cooperative than a corporation. We don’t have bosses, directors, or officers who give orders from the top down. Instead, we have trusted servants—people who volunteer to take on service roles, not to command others but to serve the group. These trusted servants are responsible to those they serve, not the other way around. They may take on roles like group secretary, treasurer, or General Service Representative (GSR), but they don’t have the authority to make unilateral decisions. Instead, decisions are made collectively by the group, often through group conscience meetings where everyone has a voice.

## Committees and Service Boards: Flexibility Over Formality

While A.A. resists formal organization, Tradition 9 does allow for the creation of service boards and committees that are directly responsible to those they serve. These boards and committees are not "leaders" in the traditional sense. They don’t dictate how groups or individuals should operate. Rather, they provide support, guidance, and administrative assistance to help A.A. function more effectively.

For example, I have served on several committees over the years, both at the group and intergroup level. These committees were responsible for organizing events, coordinating outreach efforts, or handling literature distribution. While the committees had specific tasks to accomplish, they operated in a spirit of collaboration and service, not authority. We didn’t have rigid rules, but we did have guidelines to help us stay on track and keep our focus on serving the fellowship. The beauty of Tradition 9 is that it allows for the creation of these service entities without compromising the principle of non-organization. The committees and boards exist to serve the fellowship, not to control it.

At the intergroup level, for instance, committees may be formed to plan events like conventions or retreats, or to coordinate outreach to hospitals and institutions. These committees are temporary, formed for a specific purpose, and once their job is done, they disband. The same principle applies to service boards, such as General Service Offices (GSOs) that handle administrative tasks for the wider fellowship. These boards are not in charge of A.A., but rather serve as support systems that help the fellowship stay connected and organized on a practical level.

## The Power of Personal Responsibility

Tradition 9 empowers individuals and groups to take personal responsibility for their own recovery and for the well-being of their groups. When people feel free to make their own choices, they are more likely to step up and take ownership of their role in the fellowship. This sense of personal responsibility is one of the reasons A.A. has been so successful over the years. Members are not waiting for someone to tell them what to do—they are actively engaged in their own recovery and in helping others.

This personal responsibility extends to service as well. In A.A., service is not something we do because we’re told to; it’s something we do because we want to give back. Whether it’s making coffee, chairing a meeting, or taking on a service position at the group or intergroup level, members step up because they understand the importance of contributing to the fellowship. Tradition 9 ensures that service remains voluntary, based on the principle of attraction rather than promotion. We don’t force people into service roles—they take on these roles willingly, knowing that they are helping to keep A.A. alive and thriving.

## The Genius of Non-Organization

At first, it might seem strange that A.A., a global fellowship with millions of members, resists formal organization. But in practice, this approach has proven to be one of A.A.’s greatest strengths. By allowing groups and individuals the freedom to operate without rigid control, A.A. creates an environment where personal responsibility, cooperation, and service flourish. The lack of formal organization doesn’t lead to chaos—instead, it fosters a spirit of collaboration and commitment that keeps A.A. functioning effectively.

A.A. may seem unstructured from the outside, but underneath the surface, there is an incredible amount of coordination, service, and support happening every day. This is all possible because of Tradition 9, which strikes the perfect balance between flexibility and responsibility. While A.A. may not have the traditional structure of an organization, it is far from disorganized. The service boards and committees that are created under this tradition ensure that A.A. can meet the needs of its members and carry the message of recovery to those who need it most.

## Conclusion: Freedom and Service in Harmony

Tradition 9 is a testament to the wisdom of A.A.’s founders, who understood that alcoholics don’t respond well to being controlled or dictated to. By rejecting formal organization in favor of personal responsibility and voluntary service, A.A. has created a fellowship where individuals are free to grow, contribute, and recover at their own pace. The genius of Tradition 9 is that it allows A.A. to function effectively without the need for rigid rules or a top-down hierarchy. Instead, the fellowship thrives on the willingness of its members to step up, take responsibility, and serve each other in the spirit of recovery.
