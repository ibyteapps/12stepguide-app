# Tradition 11: Our public relations policy is based on attraction rather than promotion; we need always maintain personal anonymity at the level of press, radio, and films.

Tradition 11 is one of the most important principles that has allowed Alcoholics Anonymous (A.A.) to preserve its spiritual foundation and protect the anonymity of its members. This tradition is rooted in the idea that A.A. should not engage in self-promotion or publicize individual members, but instead rely on the effectiveness of the program to attract new members. By maintaining personal anonymity at the level of press, radio, and films, A.A. ensures that the focus remains on the program itself, rather than on the personalities of individual members.

## The Birth of Anonymity in A.A.

Anonymity became a central tenet of A.A. even before the 12 Traditions were formally written down. In the early days of the fellowship, as A.A. began to gain popularity and success, many members realized that anonymity was essential to protect their privacy and the integrity of the fellowship. At that time, the press and radio were eager to report on the remarkable success stories of A.A. members who had recovered from alcoholism. While this positive publicity helped spread the word about A.A., it also raised concerns about the potential risks of public exposure for individual members.

Many early members of A.A. wanted to remain anonymous not only to protect their privacy but also to avoid being approached by the general public in their daily lives. As the fellowship grew, it became clear that a public relations policy was necessary to ensure that A.A.’s focus remained on helping alcoholics recover, rather than promoting individual members or turning them into public figures.

The early experiences of A.A. taught its founders that anonymity was crucial not just for the protection of individual members, but for the survival of the fellowship as a whole. By keeping the focus on A.A.'s principles rather than its members, the fellowship could avoid the pitfalls of ego, fame, and public scrutiny. Tradition 11 was born out of these early experiences and has since become a guiding principle for how A.A. interacts with the public.

## Attraction Rather Than Promotion

The first part of Tradition 11—"*Our public relations policy is based on attraction rather than promotion*"—captures the essence of A.A.’s approach to growth and outreach. Unlike many other organizations, A.A. does not actively promote itself or engage in marketing campaigns to recruit new members. There are no advertisements, no billboards, no public service announcements urging people to join. Instead, A.A. relies on the power of attraction—on the effectiveness of the program and the personal testimonies of its members.

When someone sees the positive changes in an A.A. member’s life—how they have stopped drinking, how their relationships have improved, how they are happier and healthier—they are naturally curious about how these changes came about. This curiosity often leads people to ask questions, and in turn, members can share their experience, strength, and hope. A.A.’s success is built on word-of-mouth and personal example, rather than on advertising or promotion.

This approach has proven to be incredibly effective. The message of A.A. spreads organically, through one alcoholic helping another. Those who are attracted to the program come because they see something they want—a solution to their drinking problem, a sense of community, a path to recovery. By focusing on attraction rather than promotion, A.A. maintains its integrity and ensures that those who come to the fellowship are genuinely seeking help, rather than being lured in by flashy advertisements or promises of quick fixes.

## The Power of Anonymity

The second part of Tradition 11—"*We need always maintain personal anonymity at the level of press, radio, and films*"—addresses the importance of protecting the anonymity of individual A.A. members in public forums. This principle ensures that no one member becomes a spokesperson for A.A., and that the fellowship remains focused on its core mission of helping alcoholics, rather than on the personalities of its members.

Anonymity serves several vital purposes. First, it protects the privacy of individual members. Many people come to A.A. at the lowest point in their lives, and they may not want the fact that they are seeking help for alcoholism to be publicly known. Anonymity allows members to recover in a safe and supportive environment without fear of public exposure or judgment.

Second, anonymity fosters humility within the fellowship. By maintaining anonymity, A.A. members are reminded that they are all equals in the fellowship, regardless of their background, status, or achievements. No one is more important than anyone else, and the focus is always on the program, not on individual members. This humility is essential to the spiritual foundation of A.A. and helps prevent the development of egos or power struggles within the fellowship.

Lastly, anonymity protects A.A. from the dangers of public scrutiny and media sensationalism. If individual members were to become public figures representing A.A., the fellowship could be drawn into controversies or scandals that could damage its reputation and effectiveness. For example, if a high-profile A.A. member were to relapse or become involved in a scandal, the public might view that as a failure of A.A. as a whole. By maintaining anonymity, A.A. ensures that no one person becomes the face of the fellowship, and that the focus remains on the collective recovery of its members.

## The Relationship Between A.A. and the Media

In the early days of A.A., the press and radio were eager to report on the success of the fellowship. Journalists were fascinated by the stories of alcoholics who had turned their lives around through A.A., and they wanted to share these stories with the public. However, A.A. quickly realized that allowing members to be publicly identified in the media could lead to problems. The fellowship had to develop a public relations policy that would protect the anonymity of its members while still allowing the media to report on its success.

Over time, the press came to respect A.A.’s commitment to anonymity. Journalists understood that A.A. was more interested in promoting its principles than its individual members. This created a relationship of mutual respect between A.A. and the media, and over the decades, journalists have reported on A.A. with great enthusiasm while honoring the tradition of anonymity.

Even today, there are occasional instances where A.A. members break their anonymity at the level of press, radio, or films. Some members may feel that the tradition is outdated or unnecessary, and they choose to go public with their affiliation to A.A. While this is their personal choice, it can have negative consequences for both the individual and the fellowship as a whole. If an A.A. member becomes a public figure and then relapses or experiences personal difficulties, it can damage the public’s perception of A.A.

## The Dangers of Fame and Ego

One notable example of the dangers of breaking anonymity is when a famous A.A. member tried to become a "mascot" for the fellowship. This individual wanted to represent A.A. publicly, believing that their fame could help spread the message of recovery. However, the founders of A.A. quickly realized that this was a risky proposition. What would happen if this person relapsed or became involved in a public scandal? In the eyes of the public, the failure of this individual could be seen as a failure of A.A. as a whole.

Tradition 11 protects A.A. from this kind of situation. By maintaining anonymity, A.A. ensures that no one person becomes the face of the fellowship, and that the program remains focused on helping alcoholics recover, not on the fame or success of its members. This tradition helps keep egos in check and ensures that the fellowship remains humble and focused on its spiritual principles.

## Anonymity in Practice

While Tradition 11 calls for anonymity at the level of press, radio, and films, it doesn’t mean that A.A. members can’t participate in public forums or share their stories. Many A.A. members have shared their experience, strength, and hope on radio shows, podcasts, and even television. However, they do so while maintaining their anonymity. This might involve using only their first names, altering their voices, or concealing their faces. In some cases, members have had their stories published in newspapers or magazines, but their identities are protected to honor the tradition.

Anonymity allows members to share their stories without fear of public exposure, and it ensures that the focus remains on the message of recovery rather than on the individual telling the story. This practice not only protects the privacy of the member but also helps keep the fellowship grounded in its spiritual principles.

## Conclusion: The Power of Anonymity and Attraction

Tradition 11 is a powerful reminder of the importance of humility, privacy, and spiritual focus within Alcoholics Anonymous. By basing its public relations policy on attraction rather than promotion, A.A. ensures that the fellowship grows organically, through personal example and word-of-mouth, rather than through advertising or public campaigns. By maintaining personal anonymity at the level of press, radio, and films, A.A. protects its members’ privacy, fosters humility, and safeguards the fellowship from the dangers of public scrutiny and media sensationalism.

For me, Tradition 11 has been a constant reminder of the importance of humility and the need to keep the focus on the program, not on myself. The art of giving is perfected through anonymity, and by maintaining my own anonymity, I am reminded that recovery is a gift that I must share with others, not for personal recognition, but because it is the right thing to do.
