# Tradition 4: Each group should be autonomous except in matters affecting other groups or A.A. as a whole.

Tradition 4 grants each Alcoholics Anonymous (A.A.) group the freedom to operate as they see fit, so long as their actions do not negatively affect other groups or A.A. as a whole. This principle of autonomy is one of the reasons A.A. has been able to grow and thrive globally, despite its diverse membership and cultural differences. While A.A. offers suggested guidelines for how groups can operate, these are not mandatory. Each group is encouraged to find what works best for them, to adapt to the needs of their members, and to create a space where recovery can flourish.

## Autonomy for Individuals and Groups

Just as A.A. grants individuals the freedom to choose how they work their program of recovery, Tradition 4 extends this same freedom to each group. When it was decided that the only requirement for A.A. membership is a desire to stop drinking (Tradition 3), it gave individuals the freedom to engage with the program in their own way. There is no mandatory attendance, no set number of meetings one must attend each week. The program is based on personal willingness and choice.

In the same spirit, Tradition 4 allows groups to operate independently. This means that groups can decide how they want to run their meetings, how often they want to meet, and what format the meetings will take. While the General Service Board in New York offers helpful suggestions based on years of collective experience, these are just that—suggestions. A.A. recognizes that what works for one group may not work for another, especially considering the vast cultural, social, and geographical differences between groups around the world. Each group is free to shape its identity and practices, provided that they do not stray so far from A.A.’s core principles that they harm other groups or the fellowship as a whole.

## The Balance of Freedom and Responsibility

While Tradition 4 grants A.A. groups great freedom, it also comes with a corresponding responsibility. With the ability to run their meetings as they see fit, groups must be mindful of how their actions affect the broader fellowship. The tradition emphasizes that while each group can make its own decisions, it should never do anything that could gravely harm A.A. as a whole. This might mean avoiding controversial or harmful practices that could damage A.A.’s reputation, alienate potential members, or cause division within the fellowship.

Another key aspect of this tradition is that groups must avoid affiliating with outside organizations or causes. A.A. groups should remain focused on their primary purpose: to stay sober and help other alcoholics achieve sobriety. When groups start aligning themselves with political, religious, or social causes, it can create division and distract from this primary mission. This tradition ensures that A.A. remains nonpartisan and nonsectarian, allowing alcoholics from all walks of life to come together in unity.

As the saying goes, "*With great power comes great responsibility*." Tradition 4 allows groups the autonomy to chart their own course, but it also reminds them that their decisions should always serve the greater good of A.A. as a whole.

## Examples of Group Autonomy in Action

Over the years, I’ve attended thousands of meetings in various countries, and I’ve witnessed firsthand the diverse ways in which different groups operate. Some groups follow A.A.’s suggested practices closely, while others have adopted unique customs based on the needs and preferences of their members. This flexibility is one of the strengths of Tradition 4—it allows groups to adapt and evolve in ways that best support their members while still adhering to A.A.’s foundational principles.

For example, I once attended a meeting called the "*No Rules Group*." Unlike many other meetings I had been to, this group did not enforce any strict guidelines about mobile phone use during meetings. While most groups discourage phone use, encouraging members to be fully present, the "*No Rules Group*" took a more relaxed approach. If someone needed to use their phone for an urgent reason, they were free to do so without fear of reprimand. In the end, it was clear that the group operated based on mutual respect, and people who knew well kept their phones away out of courtesy. Those who didn’t? Well, who was I to judge them? The group’s autonomy allowed it to foster an environment that worked for its members.

Another example is the different readings that groups choose to open their meetings. Some groups will read the "Yellow Card," while others prefer to read the "Anonymity Statement" to remind everyone of the importance of protecting each other’s privacy. While these variations might seem minor, they demonstrate the freedom each group has to tailor their meetings to fit their members’ preferences.

## Differences in Meeting Practices

I’ve also observed differences in how groups handle things like sobriety chips. Some groups give out chips to celebrate milestones in sobriety, while others do not. There has been debate in certain meetings about whether group funds (which are collected as part of Tradition 7) should be used to pay for sobriety chips. Some old-timers strongly oppose using group money for this purpose, arguing that it contradicts A.A.’s financial principles. Yet, because of Tradition 4, groups are free to make their own decisions on the matter. The autonomy granted by Tradition 4 allows these groups to continue operating, even if some members disagree on certain practices.

Similarly, I’ve attended meetings with different formats and time frames. Some meetings last as little as 45 minutes, while others go on for 2 hours. I’ve been to meetings where the secretary has less than 6 months of sobriety—despite the suggestion that the role should be held by someone with at least one year of sobriety. And I’ve even attended meetings where individuals declared themselves "recovered" from alcoholism, which sparked some debate. But again, each group has the autonomy to define what works best for them. The diversity in how meetings are run is a testament to the adaptability and inclusivity of A.A.

## Specialized Meetings and Inclusivity

One of the most fascinating aspects of Tradition 4 is how it allows for the creation of specialized groups to meet the needs of specific communities. For example, I’ve seen meetings labeled "*Women’s Meetings*" or "*LGBT Meetings*." These groups cater to specific demographics, providing a safe and supportive environment for those who may feel more comfortable sharing their experiences with others who share similar backgrounds. While some might argue that these group names imply exclusivity, Tradition 3 protects anyone’s right to attend any A.A. meeting. In reality, these meetings enhance A.A.’s inclusivity by ensuring that everyone can find a group where they feel comfortable.

## Learning from Experience: The Story of the Middleton Group

One of the most famous examples of a group testing the limits of Tradition 4 is the Middleton Group, a story recounted in the book *Twelve Steps and Twelve Traditions*. This group decided to radically depart from A.A.’s suggestions and operate in a way that eventually led to its downfall. The group’s story serves as a cautionary tale of what can happen when a group strays too far from the core principles of A.A. Without adhering to the spirit of Tradition 4—balancing autonomy with responsibility—groups risk losing the very essence of what makes A.A. effective.

## Conclusion: Rule #62 – Don’t Take Yourself Too Seriously

At the heart of Tradition 4 is the idea that A.A. is flexible, adaptable, and non-dogmatic. It’s a program that evolves based on the needs of its members, and each group has the autonomy to find its own way of carrying the message of recovery. However, this freedom must always be tempered with responsibility. Groups must remain mindful of their actions and ensure that they continue to uphold the principles that have made A.A. a place of hope and healing for millions of people around the world.

The key lesson? **Don’t take yourself too seriously**. Each group has its quirks, its differences, and its unique way of doing things. As long as the group stays committed to helping alcoholics recover, it’s doing its job.
