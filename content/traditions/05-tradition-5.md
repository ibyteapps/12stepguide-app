# Tradition 5: Each group has but one primary purpose—to carry its message to the alcoholic who still suffers.

Tradition 5 encapsulates the heart and soul of Alcoholics Anonymous (A.A.). It reminds us that no matter how we evolve as individuals or groups, our core responsibility remains the same: to carry the message of recovery to the alcoholic who still suffers. This tradition is simple yet immensely powerful. It protects A.A. groups from losing focus and ensures that the primary mission of helping alcoholics is always at the forefront. When this tradition is upheld, it has the ability to prevent the collapse of both individual members and entire groups by keeping everyone grounded in their true purpose.

## The Individual Purpose and the Group’s Role

Just as each individual member has a primary purpose to stay sober and help other alcoholics, Tradition 5 makes it clear that every group has a collective responsibility to pass on the message of recovery. This tradition ensures that no matter how much A.A. grows, or how many challenges it faces, it remains focused on helping alcoholics find sobriety. Groups are encouraged to avoid distractions, personal agendas, or outside influences that might steer them away from their primary purpose.

For me, this tradition has been a reminder to stay humble and to focus on what really matters. In my early days of sobriety, after I had a few months under my belt, I fell into the trap of thinking that I had all the answers. I started looking for ways to "fix" people—both inside and outside the fellowship. I wanted to feel good about myself by helping others, but my motives weren’t pure. It was more about feeding my ego than genuinely helping others. I even began 12-stepping people on my own, offering unsolicited advice, and pretending to be their savior. I turned up at meetings in a flashy car with the music blaring, thinking I had it all figured out.

## Avoiding Ego Trips and Staying on Course

What I eventually learned is that ego has no place in A.A. Carrying the message isn’t about being someone’s hero or proving how much I know—it’s about showing up, being of service, and helping others find the same path to recovery that I found. Tradition 5 has helped me refocus my efforts. Instead of trying to control or fix others, I’ve learned that my role is to carry the message—not to dictate how someone should live their life or recover. This tradition provides a clear framework: our job is to offer hope, to share our experience, strength, and hope, and to support others in their journey. But we must always remember that it’s up to the individual to take the steps necessary to recover.

I’ve seen how this tradition applies to groups as well. Just like individuals can get off track, so too can groups. I’ve witnessed groups that start pushing the 12 Steps too aggressively, trying to force newcomers to accept the program before they’re ready. Some groups go further and bring newcomers to religious meetings, insisting that the only way to recover is through a particular interpretation of God. While spirituality is a key element of A.A., Tradition 5 reminds us that A.A. is not affiliated with any specific religion, and we must not impose our personal beliefs on others. The message we carry is about recovery from alcoholism—nothing more, nothing less.

## Staying Focused on the Message of Recovery

Another common pitfall I’ve observed in groups is losing sight of their primary purpose during holiday seasons or special occasions. I once saw a group decide not to hold a meeting on Christmas Day, citing family obligations as more important. While family is undeniably important, I couldn’t help but think about the newcomer who might desperately need a meeting on that particular day—the person who’s sitting alone, battling the urge to drink, and desperately searching for a lifeline. Tradition 5 reminds us that the group’s primary purpose must always come first. Sobriety and recovery are the reason A.A. exists, and any decision that jeopardizes that mission should be carefully reconsidered.

I’ve also seen groups that start to drift from their primary focus by turning meetings into social gatherings or dating clubs. While it’s natural for relationships to form in A.A., especially as people begin to rebuild their lives and seek companionship, the group’s primary purpose must remain clear. Getting involved in a relationship too early in recovery—especially within the first two years—is not something most seasoned members would recommend. Such distractions can take focus away from recovery and lead to relapse. Tradition 5 keeps the group centered on its mission, ensuring that the message of recovery remains the priority.

## The Importance of Strong Home Groups

A strong home group is one that embodies Tradition 5 by staying committed to carrying the message, day in and day out. These groups remain open on their designated days and times, no matter what. They ensure that meetings are accessible to everyone, especially newcomers, and they take the responsibility of service seriously. A well-organized group will have greeters stationed outside to welcome newcomers and ensure they feel comfortable entering the meeting. They will hand out newcomer packets, provide a list of other meetings in the area, and offer support without overwhelming the newcomer.

In addition, strong home groups encourage service. Members—especially those with significant sobriety—should always be willing to 12-step new members, offering guidance and support as they navigate the early days of sobriety. However, it’s also important to recognize that not every alcoholic is ready to recover. There are those who relapse repeatedly, and while it’s important to offer them hope and support, we must also remember that we cannot force recovery on anyone. We carry the message, but it’s up to the individual to take action.

## Carrying the Message, Not the Alcoholic

One of the most valuable lessons I’ve learned from Tradition 5 is the distinction between carrying the message and carrying the alcoholic. It’s easy to fall into the trap of thinking that we are responsible for another person’s sobriety, but this is simply not true. Our role is to share our experience, strength, and hope, and to be available when someone reaches out for help. But we cannot carry someone through their recovery. Each person must take responsibility for their own sobriety, and our role is to support them on that journey—not to do the work for them.

This can be particularly challenging when dealing with individuals who are serial relapsers. I’ve seen members become frustrated when someone repeatedly comes back to meetings, only to relapse again and again. But Tradition 5 reminds us that we are here to carry the message to the alcoholic who still suffers, regardless of how many times they relapse. As long as they have a desire to stop drinking, they are welcome. But we must also protect our own sobriety by not becoming too enmeshed in someone else’s journey.

## Conclusion: Protecting the Mission of A.A.

Tradition 5 serves as a guiding principle that keeps Alcoholics Anonymous focused on its primary purpose: to carry the message of recovery to those who are still suffering. It ensures that groups don’t become distracted by outside influences or personal agendas, and it reminds individuals that their role is to share the message, not to control the outcome.

When groups and individuals honor Tradition 5, they create an environment where recovery can flourish. Meetings stay open and accessible, the focus remains on sobriety, and the fellowship continues to offer hope to those who need it most. It’s a powerful tradition that protects the integrity of A.A. and ensures that the message of recovery continues to reach those who are still suffering.

## Remember: We carry the message, not the alcoholic.
